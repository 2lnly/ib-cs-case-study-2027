Failure of a generative model to generate diverse samples

Not to be confused with [Model collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_collapse "Model collapse") or [Modal collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modal_collapse "Modal collapse").

In [machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_learning "Machine learning"), **mode collapse** is a failure mode observed in [generative models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_model "Generative model"), originally noted in [Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generative_adversarial_network "Generative adversarial network"). It occurs when the model produces outputs that are less diverse than desired undermining the goal of generative models to capture the full diversity of the training data. It usually exhibits as "collapsing to the modes". That is, the model-generated distribution is approximately just a [mixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixture_distribution "Mixture distribution") over a few [modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mode_(statistics) "Mode (statistics)") of the data distribution.

There are typically two times at which a model can collapse: either during training or during finetuning.

Mode collapse reduces the utility of generative models in applications, such as in

- image synthesis (repetitive or near-identical images);
- [data augmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_augmentation "Data augmentation") (limited diversity in [synthetic data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synthetic_data "Synthetic data"));
- scientific simulations (failure to explore all plausible scenarios).

## Distinctions

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Mode_collapse&action=edit&section=1 "Edit section: Distinctions")\]

Mode collapse is distinct from [overfitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overfitting "Overfitting"), where a model learns detailed patterns in the training data that do not generalize to the test data, and [underfitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underfitting "Underfitting"), where it fails to learn patterns. The distribution generated by an overfitting model tends to have low [divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divergence_(statistics) "Divergence (statistics)") from the training dataset distribution, but high divergence from the test dataset distribution. A mode-collapsed model tends to achieve high divergence to both. An underfitting model tends to not generate any pattern accurately, but a mode-collapsed model tends to generate a few patterns accurately.

[Memorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memorization_(artificial_intelligence) "Memorization (artificial intelligence)") is where a model learns to reproduce data from the training data. Memorization is often confused with mode collapse. However, a model can memorize the training dataset without mode collapse. Indeed, if a model is severely mode-collapsed, then it has failed to memorize large parts of the training dataset.

[Model collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_collapse "Model collapse") is one particular mechanism for the phenomenon of mode collapse, i.e. when a generative model 2 is pretrained mainly on the outputs of model 1, then another new generative model 3 is pretrained mainly on the outputs of model 2, etc. When models are trained in this way, each model is typically more mode-collapsed than the previous one. However, there are other mechanisms for mode collapse.

## In GANs

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Mode_collapse&action=edit&section=2 "Edit section: In GANs")\]

Training-time mode collapse was originally noted and studied in GANs, where it arises primarily due to imbalances in the training dynamics between the generator and discriminator in GANs. In the original GAN paper, it was also called the "[Helvetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helvetica "Helvetica") scenario".^([\[1\]](#cite_note-1)[\[2\]](#cite_note-2))

Common causes include:^([\[3\]](#cite_note-3))

- If the discriminator learns too slowly, the generator may exploit weaknesses by producing a narrow set of outputs that consistently fool the discriminator.
- Traditional GAN loss functions (e.g., [Jensen–Shannon divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jensen–Shannon_divergence "Jensen–Shannon divergence")) may be too lenient on generating same-looking outputs.
- The adversarial training process can lead to oscillatory behavior, where the generator and discriminator fail to converge to a stable equilibrium, but instead engage in a rock-beats-paper-beats-scissors kind of cycling. The generator would generate just "rock" until the discriminator learns to classify that as generated, then the generator switch to generating just "scissors", and so on. The generator would always be mode-collapsed, though the precise mode in which it collapses to would change during training.

Several GAN-specific strategies were developed to mitigate mode collapse:

- Two time-scale update rule.^([\[4\]](#cite_note-4))
- Mini-batch discrimination^([\[5\]](#cite_note-5)) allows the discriminator to evaluate entire batches of samples, encouraging diversity.
- Unrolled GANs^([\[6\]](#cite_note-6)) optimize the generator against future states of the discriminator.
- [Wasserstein GAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wasserstein_GAN "Wasserstein GAN") uses [Earth Mover's distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_mover's_distance "Earth mover's distance") to provide more stable gradients.^([\[7\]](#cite_note-7))
- Use a big and balanced training dataset.^([\[8\]](#cite_note-8))
- [Regularization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regularization_(mathematics) "Regularization (mathematics)") methods such as gradient penalty and [spectral normalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalization_(machine_learning) "Normalization (machine learning)").^([\[9\]](#cite_note-9))

## Finetuning

\[[edit](/w/index.php?title=Mode_collapse&action=edit&section=3 "Edit section: Finetuning")\]

The [large language models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model "Large language model") are usually trained in two steps. In the first step ("pretraining"), the model is trained to simply generate text sampled from a large dataset. In the second step ("finetuning"), the model is trained to perform specific tasks by training it on a small dataset containing just the task-specific data. For example, to make a chatbot in this method, one first pretrains a large [transformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer_(deep_learning_architecture) "Transformer (deep learning architecture)") model over a few trillion words of text scraped from the Internet, then finetunes it on a few million words of example chatlogs that the model should imitate.

Mode collapse may occur during finetuning, as the model learns to generate text that accomplishes the specific task, but loses ability to generate other forms of text. It may also be able to generate a smaller subset of texts that accomplish the specific task. It is hypothesized that there is a tradeoff between quality and diversity. Given a single pretrained model, one may finetune it to perform a specific task. More finetuning would result in higher average task performance, but less diverse outputs. Less finetuning would result in lower average performance, but more diverse outputs.^([\[10\]](#cite_note-10)) A similar tradeoff has been observed in image generation models^([\[11\]](#cite_note-11)) and GAN-based text generators.^([\[12\]](#cite_note-12))

Similarly, mode collapse may occur during [RLHF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforcement_learning_from_human_feedback "Reinforcement learning from human feedback"), via [reward hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reward_hacking "Reward hacking") the reward model or other mechanisms.^([\[13\]](#cite_note-13)[\[14\]](#cite_note-14))
